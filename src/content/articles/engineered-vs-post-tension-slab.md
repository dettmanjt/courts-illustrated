This is one of the questions I hear most: do we need an engineered slab, or is a standard court slab fine?

For most courts, a standard post-tension slab built to the ASBA non-engineered standard is the right answer. An engineered slab makes sense when the soil is bad enough that it has to be dealt with, or when someone needs an engineer's stamp. The way to know is a geotech report.

## What a standard post-tension court slab does

The ASBA non-engineered standard is a post-tension slab built to resist shrinkage cracking. That's the main job of post-tension on a court: keep cracks down.

The cables are stressed after the pour, and they pull the whole slab toward its center. That compression is what fights the cracking you'd get in a plain slab. It's also why you never want a post-tension slab anchored to anything at the edge. An anchor point becomes a crack point.

If the ground under a standard slab ever heaves badly, you treat that like foundation repair. It's not the slab failing at its job. It's the soil doing something the slab wasn't designed to fix.

## What a geotech report tells you

A geotechnical report comes from core samples drilled into the soil, about 20 feet down on a tennis rebuild we priced. The report measures how much the soil can rise, often expressed as PVR (potential vertical rise).

That number drives the decision. On one recent residential job, the geotech showed a PVR of 1 to 1.25 inches. That's fine for a small slab, and a standard post-tension court made sense.

## Two ways an engineer handles bad soil

When the soil is bad, engineers generally take one of two routes.

**Fix the soil.** The engineer wants PVR under about an inch. That can mean digging out and replacing feet of soil. It works, but the dirt work can get expensive fast, and nobody can price it until the drilling is done.

**Stiffen the slab.** The engineer adds interior beams every four or five feet so the slab can't move. Structurally, that works. But it doesn't do what post-tension is for on a court, which is keeping cracks down. You can end up with a slab that doesn't move but still cracks.

## When treating the soil costs more than the court

This is the part people don't expect. Sometimes treating the soil would cost more than the whole slab.

In that case, the math often favors a standard post-tension slab and accepting that you might replace it someday, rather than paying today for soil work that costs more than the court. That isn't cutting corners. It's choosing to spend money where it does the most good.

There's also a middle path for existing courts: an unengineered post-tension cap. You keep the old base, add a couple of inches of sand as a cushion, and pour a new post-tension slab over the top so the old slab can move underneath. It doesn't fix the soil, but it's a very good option for courts that keep cracking.

## How to decide

Here's how I'd walk through it:

1. Get a geotech report if there's any doubt about your soil.
2. If PVR is modest, a standard post-tension slab is usually right.
3. If PVR is high, get an engineer's recommendation and a price for the soil work.
4. Compare that price to the cost of the slab itself. If the soil work costs more than the court, talk honestly with your builder about whether it's worth it.
5. If you need an engineer's stamp for a board, a lender or a city, that decides it for you.

## The bottom line

Most courts don't need an engineered slab. They need a well-built post-tension slab and a builder who checks the soil before pouring. When the soil really is bad, an engineered slab is the right tool, but look at what the soil work costs first. Sometimes the smartest money is the money you don't spend.
