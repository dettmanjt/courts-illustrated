An HOA board told us their tennis courts crack again within a year of every resurfacing. They wanted to know: do we tear them out or pour over them?

Here's the honest answer. There are three ways to fix courts like that. The cheapest one won't stop the cracks. The middle one is a very good option for most courts. The top one is the only one that deals with the soil itself, and it costs the most and takes the most planning.

## Option 1: Resurface over what's there

This is the cheapest option. We fill and patch the cracks, then put new acrylic coatings over the existing slab.

It looks great when we leave. But if the slab underneath is moving, the cracks come back. That's what this board had been living with: pay to resurface, watch the cracks return, repeat.

Resurfacing makes sense when the slab is sound and you're dealing with worn color, not a slab that keeps breaking. If your courts crack every year, resurfacing alone is treating the symptom.

## Option 2: Unengineered post-tension cap

This is the one I'd look at first for most cracking courts.

We keep the old base where it is. We add a couple of inches of sand on top as a cushion, so the old slab can heave and settle underneath without dragging the new court with it. Then we pour a new 5-inch post-tension slab on top.

Post-tension cables pull the whole slab toward its center when they're stressed. That's what keeps shrinkage cracking down, and it's why this approach works so well on courts.

One important detail: a post-tension slab shouldn't be anchored to anything. If you pin the edge, for example with a turned-down lip over old concrete or piers tied into the slab, that spot becomes an anchor point and the slab cracks there as it shrinks. Shore up the soil outside the court instead, and let the new slab float over the old one.

The tradeoff: this option does nothing about the soil. If the ground under your courts moves a lot, the cap rides it out better than old concrete, but the soil is still doing what it does.

## Option 3: Fully engineered post-tension

This is the top tier, and it starts with a geotech report.

A geotechnical firm drills core samples about 20 feet down to measure how much the soil can rise. Then an engineer decides how much dirt has to be dug out and replaced, and designs the slab.

Here's something buyers should know up front: we can price everything except that dirt work until the drilling is done. Nobody can honestly tell you what soil replacement will cost before the report comes back.

What you get at the end is a slab with an engineer's stamp that performs for decades.

## Which one do you need?

A quick way to think about it:

- **Worn surface, solid slab:** resurface.
- **Cracks that keep coming back, and you want a durable court without a big soil project:** unengineered post-tension cap.
- **You need an engineer's stamp, or the soil is the real problem and you want it solved:** fully engineered post-tension.

For many HOAs, the cap is the sweet spot. Sometimes treating the soil would cost more than the whole slab, and replacing a slab years from now would be cheaper. A geotech report is what tells you which side of that line you're on.

If your board is collecting bids, ask every contractor which of these three they're proposing. A resurfacing bid and a post-tension cap bid are not the same project, even if both say "court repair."

## The bottom line

If your courts crack every year, another resurfacing will most likely give you another year. A post-tension cap over the old courts is a very good fix for most boards. A fully engineered slab is the right call when you need the soil solved and the stamp to prove it, but get the geotech report first so you know what you're paying for.
