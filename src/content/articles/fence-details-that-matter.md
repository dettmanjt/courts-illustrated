Fencing looks like the simple part of a court. It isn't. A few small choices decide whether your fence protects players or hurts them, and whether your slab stays crack-free at the edge.

Here are the three I care about most: use round posts, include a top rail, and set the posts about 4 inches in from the slab edge.

## Round posts, not square

The USTA said long ago that court posts should be round, and pretty much every sports construction standard uses round posts.

The reason is simple. If someone runs into a post or hits their head on it, they hit a blunt, rounded surface instead of a corner. Players run hard and look at the ball, not the fence. Sooner or later, someone makes contact.

Square posts can look sharp on a residential design, and I understand why landscape architects sometimes like them. But they usually need padding to be safe, and padding is one more thing to buy, maintain and replace.

## Get a top rail

A top rail runs along the top of the fence and ties the posts together.

Without one, the fence bends when players run into it. Over time you get a wavy, sagging fence that looks bad and doesn't protect anyone well.

It's a small cost compared to the fence itself, and it's one of the first things I'd check on any bid.

## Set posts about 4 inches in from the slab edge

This one surprises people. Why not put the posts right at the edge of the concrete?

Because posts right at the edge crack the concrete, especially on post-tension slabs. Setting posts right at the edge invites cracking.

We set posts about 4 inches in. That puts the fence face about 6 inches in from the edge.

There's a safety reason too. With the fence set in, there's no gap between the fence and a drop at the slab edge where someone could roll an ankle.

And one finishing detail: slope the fence top with the slab. Courts are built with a slight slope for drainage, and if the fence follows it, your eye won't read the fence as crooked.

## Windscreen: don't turn the fence into a sail

If you add windscreen, keep it in proportion. A standard fence with windscreen is built for roughly 50 mph winds. Run the windscreen too high and it catches wind like a sail and can fold the fence.

That's why screens are tied on loosely. Before a forecast wind event, cut the ties and fold the screen in half.

## What to look for on a bid

When you're comparing fence pricing, ask:

- Are the posts round?
- Is there a top rail?
- How far in from the slab edge are the posts set?
- How high does the windscreen go, and how is it attached?

If two bids look far apart on fencing, these details are often the reason.

## The bottom line

A good court fence uses round posts so players hit a blunt surface, a top rail so it doesn't bend, and posts set about 4 inches in so the slab edge doesn't crack and nobody rolls an ankle. None of these add much cost. Skipping them can cost a lot.
