An HOA board asked us a smart question: can we pour the slab now so a shade structure or pavilion can be added later? And can't you just drill the posts in afterward?

Yes, you can plan for shade later, but you have to build for it now. No, you shouldn't plan on drilling posts into a plain slab afterward. Drilling posts into a plain 4-inch slab later cracks the edges off.

The easiest way to explain it is that there are really three tiers of concrete construction.

## Tier 1: Patio or driveway

This is the lowest cost. It's a slab with nothing structural on it: a driveway, a patio, a flat surface to walk, drive or play on.

It does that job well. What it isn't built to do is hold up columns. Concentrated loads from posts, plus the wind load on a shade canopy, are a different kind of stress than people walking around.

## Tier 2: Pergola-ready

This is the middle tier, and it's the one I want boards to know about.

A pergola-ready slab is built to hold a light structure like a pergola or shade canopy. It adds:

- Extra reinforcement
- An interior beam
- A footer, 2 feet by 2 feet, wherever a column will land

It adds very little cost compared to the patio tier. And it means that when the budget for shade comes through, you set columns on footers that were designed for them, instead of cutting into a slab that wasn't.

The catch: you need to know roughly where the columns will go. That means at least a rough plan for the future structure before we pour.

## Tier 3: Foundation

The top tier is a house foundation. It's engineered, and it's a different world of cost.

For a court with a shade structure, you almost never need this tier. I mention it so you understand the range. When someone hears "build it to hold a structure," they sometimes imagine foundation pricing. That's not what a pergola-ready slab is.

## Why drilling later doesn't work

It seems like it should be simple: pour now, drill and anchor posts later. Here's the problem.

A plain 4-inch slab doesn't have reinforcement or a footer where your post lands. Anchoring a column near the edge of that slab, with the loads a shade structure puts on it, cracks the edges off. Then you're repairing concrete before you even get to enjoy the shade.

On a post-tension court slab, there's another reason to plan ahead. Post-tension slabs shouldn't be anchored to things that pin them in place, because those spots become crack points as the slab shrinks. Your builder needs to plan where and how any structure ties in.

## What to ask your builder

If shade is even a maybe, ask:

- Can you price the slab pergola-ready, with footers at future column locations?
- What do you need from us about the future structure to do that?
- How does that work with a post-tension court slab?

Get it priced as a separate line item so the board can see exactly what the upgrade costs.

## The bottom line

If there's any real chance you'll add shade, build the middle tier now. A pergola-ready slab adds very little cost and saves you from cracked edges and repairs later. If you're sure you'll never add a structure, a standard slab is fine, and there's no reason to pay for something you won't use.
