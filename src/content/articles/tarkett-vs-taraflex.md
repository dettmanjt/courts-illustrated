A volleyball facility owner in Lubbock told us the club wanted Taraflex. Fair enough. It's the floor you hear announcers mention at Olympic and college volleyball. So the question we had to answer was simple: is there a better or less expensive floor?

My short answer: Taraflex is a good product. On the technical details that matter most to an owner, the Tarkett floor we compared it against holds up as well or better, and it's usually less expensive. But you shouldn't take my word for it, so here's how to compare them yourself.

## Why "2 mm" can mislead you

This is the one that trips up owners and architects the most.

A sport vinyl floor is built in layers. On top is a clear wear layer. Under that is the printed wood grain or color. Below that are intermediate layers with a fiberglass grid, and then foam.

The ASTM standards for this (F410 and F1303) count only the clear layer above the print as the wear layer. Tarkett reports 0.7 mm measured that way. It also lists its full compact layer, meaning everything above the foam, at 2.0 mm.

Gerflor's Taraflex sheet lists a 2.1 mm "wearlayer" and leaves the ASTM column blank. That 2.1 mm is really the compact layer, not the ASTM wear layer.

So when you set the two sheets side by side, you see 2 next to 0.7 and assume 2 is better. It isn't the same measurement. Ask both manufacturers for the F410/F1303 number and compare those. For reference, commercial grade needs about 0.51 mm or more.

If you want certainty, Tarkett has offered to pay for an independent lab, chosen by the customer, to test both samples. I like that offer, because it takes the sales talk out of it.

## Shock absorption: same class means same on paper

ASTM F2772 groups floors into classes so buyers don't get stuck comparing single data points like "21% beats 20%." Class 3 covers 34 to 45% force reduction.

Both floors we compared, Tarkett Omnisports Active+ and Taraflex Sport M Plus, are Class 3.

You may see a "45%" figure online for Sport M Plus. Gerflor's own sheet says 34% to under 39%, so be careful with that one. Tarkett doesn't publish its exact number. My honest advice: they're in the same class. Treat them as equal on shock absorption and decide on other things.

## The warranty is the headline difference

Moisture coming up through the slab is historically the biggest killer of glued vinyl floors. That makes the moisture warranty the most important line on the page.

Here's what we were told and what the comparison documents show. Tarkett offers 25 years covering product defect, wear layer and moisture tolerance. According to the Tarkett rep, if the floor fails, Tarkett pays labor to remove and replace it, not just new material. Gerflor offers a 15-year limited warranty, with no moisture warranty on its moisture options, and replacement covers material only.

Before you sign anything, read the actual warranty documents for both. Warranty language is where details hide, and you want them in writing, not from a salesperson (including me).

## Moisture systems on a damp slab

Even in dry West Texas, the facility asked if they really needed a moisture system. In our experience, yes.

For a slab at 100% relative humidity, compare Tarkett's Tarkolay against Gerflor's Dry-Tex, not against standard glue. Dry-Tex is fully adhered, so slab cracks and joint movement can telegraph through. Tarkolay floats over a moisture barrier and lets the slab breathe, bridging minor cracks and control joints.

We learned this the hard way. A decade ago we installed a lot of a perimeter-glue system (GreenLay) in Texas. It's limited to 92% RH, and it failed as slab moisture rose. Today we recommend a 98% RH full-spread system as the entry option, and the 100% floating system as preferred, even in Lubbock.

## Cleaning, and the finger test

Every sport vinyl is embossed for traction and to cut glare. Tarkett uses a nonlinear emboss with no pits, so scuffs from black-soled shoes have nowhere to collect and an auto scrubber cleans it. Linear, pitted embossing traps dirt and gives older gyms a "five o'clock shadow."

You don't need a spec sheet for this one. Get both samples and rub a finger across them.

## The bottom line

Don't buy a volleyball floor on brand recognition, and don't buy it on a single number. Compare the ASTM wear layer, confirm both floors are in the same shock class, read both warranties for moisture and labor coverage, and pick the right moisture system for your slab. If you'd still rather have Taraflex after all that, it's a good floor. Just make the choice with the real numbers in front of you.
