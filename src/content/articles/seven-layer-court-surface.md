When a spec or a sales sheet says "seven layers," it usually means one thing: a rubber cushion glued to the slab under the acrylic.

My honest take: outdoors, for most people, I'd skip it. If you're serious about pickleball, go hard acrylic straight on concrete. If the court is for grandkids on trikes and skates and you want something softer, modular tile gives you the cushion without the long-term mess.

Here's why.

## What a standard court surface is

A standard hard court is about five layers:

1. Binder
2. A black layer
3. A first color coat
4. A second color coat
5. Line striping

That's a hard acrylic system, applied straight onto the concrete.

## What the extra layers are

A "seven-layer" system adds a rubber cushion layer, up to about 5 mm thick, glued to the slab before the acrylic goes on top.

In year one, it's great. It feels soft underfoot, and it hides shrinkage cracks in the slab. I understand why people like it when they walk on a new one.

## What it looks like in year ten

Outdoors, that rubber ages like an old tire. It hardens. Then it cracks. And the fix is not a simple recoat. It means stripping the rubber off with a floor scraper before you can resurface.

You should expect to be dealing with that in about 10 years.

## The cost and what you actually get

Here's the tradeoff in plain numbers from our experience:

- The cushion adds roughly 10 to 12% shock absorption.
- It about triples the cost of the paint system.
- It brings a removal job in about 10 years.

So you're paying a lot more up front for a modest gain in comfort, and you're signing up for a messier, more expensive refresh later. For a lot of buyers, that's not a good trade.

## When cushion does make sense

I'm not saying nobody should want a softer surface. It depends on who's using the court.

- **Serious pickleball players:** hard acrylic on concrete. It plays consistently, refreshes with normal resurfacing, and you skip the rubber removal.
- **Grandkids, trikes, skates and family play:** modular tile is worth a look. It gives you cushion without gluing rubber to your slab.
- **Indoors:** that's a different conversation with different products. This article is about outdoor courts.

If a bid you're looking at says "seven layers" or "cushioned," ask what the extra layers are made of, how thick they are, and what the plan is when they wear out. A good builder will answer that without hesitating.

## The bottom line

More layers doesn't mean a better court. Outdoors, a rubber cushion under acrylic feels great at first, costs about three times as much in the paint system, and leaves you with a scraping job in about a decade. Hard acrylic straight on concrete is the right call for most courts, and modular tile is the better way to get cushion if you really need it.
